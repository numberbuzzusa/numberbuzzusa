How Much Tax Do You Pay on Lottery Winnings? (2026 Guide)

Lottery Guide · NumberBuzz USA

Here is the short answer: if you win a big US lottery prize, the IRS takes a mandatory 24% federal withholding off the top before you receive anything, and because lottery winnings are taxed as ordinary income at rates up to 37%, you will very likely owe more when you file your return. Most states then take their own cut — anywhere from 0% to over 10% — and a city tax may apply on top. A $1 million prize is therefore never a $1 million payday. This guide explains how the pieces fit together. It is general information only, not tax advice.

Step one: the 24% withholding

For lottery prizes over $5,000, the lottery must withhold 24% for federal taxes at the moment it pays you. This is not your final tax bill — it is a down payment. Think of it exactly like the withholding on a paycheck: the real calculation happens when you file your tax return for the year you receive the prize. Winners who provide no taxpayer ID number can face “backup withholding” at the same 24% rate, and non-US persons are generally withheld at 30%.

Step two: your real federal rate can reach 37%

Lottery winnings are ordinary income, stacked on top of everything else you earn. A jackpot-sized prize pushes you straight into the top federal bracket — 37% on income above roughly $640,000 for single filers (the threshold is indexed yearly). In practice, that means a lump-sum winner should expect to owe an additional chunk beyond the 24% withheld — often another 10–13% of the prize — when the return is filed. Sensible winners park that money untouched in a separate account the day the prize arrives. Step three: state taxes — the great lottery lottery

Where you bought the ticket matters enormously. A handful of states do not tax lottery winnings at all — California famously exempts its state lottery prizes from state income tax, and states with no income tax (such as Florida, Texas, Washington, Nevada and Tennessee) take nothing either. At the other end, states like New York withhold over 10%, and New York City residents pay an extra city tax of up to about 3.9% on top. Most states fall somewhere between roughly 3% and 8%. Note that you generally owe the tax to the state where the ticket was bought, even if you live elsewhere — and your home state may also want a share, usually with a credit for tax paid to the other state.

Lump sum vs annuity: the tax difference

Taking the cash option means the entire (smaller) lump sum is taxed in one year — one giant spike into the top bracket. Taking the annuity spreads 30 payments over 30 years; each payment is taxed in the year it arrives, which can keep some payments in lower brackets and lets future tax law changes cut either way. The annuity’s total is larger, but after tax and inflation the comparison is closer than the headline numbers suggest. This is a decision to model with a tax professional, not a rule of thumb.

Smaller prizes and paperwork

Prizes of $600 or more are reported to the IRS on Form W-2G. Prizes above $5,000 trigger the automatic 24% withholding. And if you play in a group — an office pool, for example — the IRS Form 5754 lets the lottery split the prize and the tax paperwork among members, which is far cleaner than one person collecting and sharing afterwards.

Sharing the win: gifts, groups and paperwork

If you plan to share a prize with family, the tax paperwork matters as much as the money. Giving relatives a share after collecting it yourself can trigger gift-tax rules and leaves the full income tax on you. Claiming as a group from the start — with Form 5754 for pools, or a properly drawn trust or partnership for family — splits both the money and the tax bill correctly from day one. This is one more reason the lawyer comes before the claim, not after.

Frequently asked questions

How much tax do you pay on a $1 million lottery win?

Federal withholding takes $240,000 immediately, and depending on your total income you may owe up to roughly $130,000 more at filing time. Add state tax (0% to 10%+) and a $1 million prize typically lands somewhere between about $600,000 and $760,000 after tax.

Do all states tax lottery winnings?

No. California does not tax state lottery prizes, and states without an income tax take nothing. Most other states tax winnings at their normal income tax rates.

Is the 24% withholding my final tax?

No — it is only a prepayment. Your final federal bill is calculated on your tax return, and for large prizes it usually comes to more than 24%.
