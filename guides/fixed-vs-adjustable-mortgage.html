Fixed vs Adjustable Mortgage Rates: What’s the Difference?

Money Guide · NumberBuzz USA

The difference in one sentence: a fixed-rate mortgage keeps the same interest rate — and the same principal-and-interest payment — for the entire loan, while an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) starts with a lower fixed rate for a set number of years and then resets periodically to follow market rates, so your payment can rise or fall. Which one suits you depends mainly on how long you will keep the loan and how much payment uncertainty your budget can absorb. NumberBuzz tracks today’s average mortgage rates every morning; this guide explains the mechanics. It is general information, not financial advice.

How a fixed-rate mortgage works

With a fixed-rate loan — most commonly 30 years or 15 years — the rate you lock at closing is the rate you die with (or refinance out of). Your monthly principal-and-interest payment never changes, which makes budgeting simple and protects you completely if market rates climb. The trade-offs: fixed rates usually start higher than ARM rates, and if market rates fall, the only way to benefit is to refinance, with its own closing costs. The 15-year version carries a notably lower rate and builds equity far faster, at the cost of a much higher monthly payment.

How an ARM works

ARMs are described with two numbers, like 5/1 or 7/6. The first is the number of years the initial rate is fixed; the second is how often the rate adjusts afterwards (every 1 year for a 5/1, every 6 months for a 7/6). After the fixed period, the new rate is calculated as a published index (today usually SOFR) plus a set margin. Protections called caps limit the damage: a typical structure caps the first adjustment at 2%, each later adjustment at 2%, and the lifetime increase at 5% above the starting rate. Even so, a borrower who keeps an ARM past its fixed period must be ready for the maximum payment the caps allow.

The real deciding question: how long will you keep it?

Most mortgages do not live to old age — people move, refinance or pay off early, and the average loan lasts well under ten years. If you are confident you will sell or refinance within five to seven years, an ARM’s lower starting rate can save real money, and you may never face an adjustment at all. If this is your long-term home, or if a payment jump of several hundred dollars would break your budget, the fixed rate buys certainty that most borrowers find worth its premium. Run the numbers both ways over your expected horizon, not the loan’s full 30 years.

Watch the details, not just the rate

Compare the APR, not only the interest rate — it folds in many lender fees and is the fairer comparison. On an ARM, ask for the index, the margin, all three caps, and the worst-case payment in writing. On any loan, check for prepayment penalties (rare in modern US mortgages, but ask), and remember your full monthly cost also includes property tax, homeowner’s insurance and possibly mortgage insurance, which the lender’s rate quote does not show. Interest-only wrinkles and who should avoid them

Some ARMs add an interest-only period, where early payments cover no principal at all — the balance does not fall, and the eventual fully-amortizing payment jumps sharply. These loans exist for specific cash-flow situations (irregular income, a certain near-term sale), but for a typical household buying a home to live in, they stack one risk on top of another. If a lender leads with the lowest possible payment rather than the rate structure, slow down and read the caps page twice.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 15-year fixed mortgage better than a 30-year?

It costs less overall — lower rate, far less total interest — and builds equity much faster, but the monthly payment is roughly 40–50% higher. It suits borrowers with comfortable cash flow who value being mortgage-free sooner.

Can an ARM payment double?

The caps prevent a sudden doubling in normal structures: with 2% periodic caps, the rate climbs in steps, and the lifetime cap (often 5–6 points above the start rate) sets the ceiling. Always ask for the maximum possible payment figure before signing.

Why are ARM rates sometimes barely lower than fixed rates?

When markets expect rates to fall, lenders price fixed loans aggressively and the ARM advantage shrinks; when rates are expected to rise, the gap widens. The spread between the two is itself a market forecast.
